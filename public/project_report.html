ABSTRACT

Foliar plant diseases represent a critical threat to global agricultural food security, reducing crop yields by an estimated 20% to 40% annually across major horticultural staples. In smallholder farming communities across developing regions, visual disease identification is hampered by a lack of accessible agronomic specialists, laboratory facilities, and visual mimicry among fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens.

This project presents an integrated, edge-deployable, multi-crop plant leaf disease classification and farmer advisory platform. The system supports three economically vital regional crops—Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), Potato (Solanum tuberosum), and Chilli Pepper (Capsicum annuum)—encompassing twenty-one distinct pathology and healthy foliage categories. The platform translates recent advances in deep learning into an accessible Progressive Web Application (PWA) operating directly within modern smartphone and desktop web browsers.

The system architecture combines three specialized neural classification models executed via ONNX Runtime WebAssembly (WASM): a fine-tuned MobileNetV3 V2 compound architecture for tomato pathology (10 classes), an optimized CNN for potato blights (3 classes), and a dedicated classifier for chilli infections and nutritional deficiencies (8 classes). To guard against unreliable field predictions, the platform implements a multi-stage input validation pipeline incorporating Laplacian blur estimation, luminance checking, and softmax entropy-based uncertainty calibration with a 60% confidence threshold gating mechanism. Predictions are matched with actionable agricultural advisory protocols detailing cultural management, organic remedies, and approved chemical treatments.

The platform was empirically evaluated using standardized datasets and curated photographic reference libraries. On validation datasets, the MobileNetV3 V2 tomato classifier achieved 99.56% accuracy on clean benchmark samples and 89.4% macro-averaged validation accuracy across multi-crop field conditions. System latency testing demonstrated sub-second inference execution (110–180 ms) on consumer mobile devices without server dependency or data transmission costs, demonstrating the viability of private, edge-native AI for frontline precision agriculture.

INTRODUCTION

1.1 Overview

Foliar plant diseases represent one of the most critical threats to global agricultural stability, reducing annual crop production by an estimated 20% to 40% across major commercial food staples worldwide [1]. In developing and emerging agrarian economies, smallholder farming operations sustain rural livelihoods and supply the majority of domestic produce. However, these agricultural ecosystems face persistent vulnerability to pathogenic infections caused by fungal spores, bacterial blights, and viral vectors.

Horticultural crops such as Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), Potato (Solanum tuberosum), and Chilli Pepper (Capsicum annuum) are economically indispensable yet physiologically susceptible to rapid disease progression. When pathogen outbreaks occur, early visual symptoms frequently manifest as localized foliar lesions, chlorotic halos, or structural leaf curling. For example, early blight caused by Alternaria solani produces concentric circular target-like spots, while late blight caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans initiates dark, water-soaked foliar necrosis that can destroy entire acreage within seven to ten days under cool, humid weather conditions [1].

Traditional disease identification relies almost exclusively on manual physical inspection by experienced agricultural extension officers or destructive laboratory assays. In typical agricultural belts, extension officers must serve thousands of farming households spread across vast geographic territories, resulting in critical diagnostic delays of several weeks. By the time a definitive diagnosis is confirmed, fungal or viral pathogens have frequently spread to neighboring fields.

Furthermore, visual diagnosis in the field is complicated by extensive visual mimicry: different pathogens often produce overlapping early-stage necrotic spots, while distinct diseases may exhibit similar chlorosis patterns. Under-resourced farmers frequently respond with broad-spectrum prophylactic chemical spraying. This indiscriminate application of chemical fungicides not only increases input costs and erodes operating margins, but also induces environmental chemical runoff, pollutes rural groundwater tables, and accelerates pathogen resistance.

1.2 Motivation and Objective

Despite the rapid proliferation of computer vision research in precision agriculture, existing diagnostic approaches present critical limitations that prevent effective frontline adoption. First, the vast majority of existing academic models are trained and evaluated strictly on clean laboratory benchmarks such as PlantVillage [1], where leaves are photographed against uniform, monochromatic backgrounds under controlled illumination. When deployed in real agricultural fields, these models suffer severe performance degradation—termed the laboratory-to-field domain gap—due to dynamic natural lighting, soil clutter, and leaf occlusions [4, 7].

Second, existing agricultural tools typically require continuous cloud connectivity, transmitting heavy uncompressed images to distant server clusters. In rural farming regions where cellular connectivity is intermittent, slow, or expensive, cloud-tethered architectures fail to deliver reliable diagnostics. Third, standard deep learning classifiers produce raw softmax probability distributions that are notoriously overconfident, assigning high certainty scores to out-of-domain weeds or unreadable blurry images.

2.1 Deep Learning for Plant Pathology

2.1.1 Foundational CNN Architectures on Benchmark Datasets

Introduction: The seminal investigation by Mohanty, Hughes, and Salathé [1] (Frontiers in Plant Science, 2016) demonstrated the transformative potential of deep convolutional neural networks for automated plant disease diagnosis. The authors evaluated standard architectures on the open-access PlantVillage repository.

Approach: The authors implemented AlexNet and GoogLeNet backbones, comparing performance when trained from scratch against transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained weights across color, grayscale, and segmented leaf representations.

Dataset and Setup: The study evaluated 54,306 images spanning 14 crop species and 26 diseases. A standard 80–20 train-test split was applied, with stochastic gradient descent optimization.

Results: The fine-tuned GoogLeNet model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 99.35% on held-out test data. However, when tested on leaf photographs collected independently from online sources outside PlantVillage, accuracy plummeted below 31%.

Conclusion and Limitations: The authors concluded that deep learning achieves outstanding classification under controlled conditions, but cautioned that models learn background correlations rather than pure foliar pathology, necessitating real-world field evaluations.

2.1.2 Deep Residual Learning and Multi-Scale Extraction

Introduction: Too et al. [2] (PeerJ Computer Science, 2019) conducted a comparative architectural evaluation across advanced convolutional networks, including VGG-16, Inception-V4, ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and DenseNet-121, for crop pathology identification.

Approach: The authors investigated gradient flow and feature reuse in deep residual networks, evaluating how identity mappings and dense feature concatenations mitigate vanishing gradients during fine-tuning on fine-grained foliar lesions.

Dataset and Setup: Experiments utilized 54,305 PlantVillage leaf images covering 38 distinct crop-disease classes under standardized laboratory settings.

Results: DenseNet-121 achieved the highest top-1 classification accuracy of 99.75%, closely followed by ResNet-50 at 99.53%. DenseNet exhibited superior parameter efficiency compared to VGG-16 while demonstrating faster epoch convergence.

Conclusion and Limitations: Modern residual and densely connected backbones provide superior feature extraction for fine foliar spots. However, the models remained computationally heavy (>80 MB weight footprints), restricting their deployment on edge smartphones without quantization.

2.1.3 Transfer Learning from General-Domain Ensembles

Introduction: Ferentinos [3] (Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, 2018) investigated deep learning convolutional neural network models for plant disease detection using an extensive multi-crop database of healthy and diseased leaves.

Approach: Five prominent architectures (VGG, VGG-CNN-S, VGG-CNN-M, VGG-CNN-F, and AlexNet) were evaluated with diverse transfer learning protocols. The study examined the impact of laboratory versus field imagery.

Dataset and Setup: A composite database of 87,848 images covering 58 distinct classes across 25 crop species was compiled.

Results: The VGG architecture achieved 99.53% operational classification accuracy on the combined test set. The author demonstrated that training on mixed datasets substantially improved model robustness compared to training strictly on isolated laboratory imagery.

Conclusion and Limitations: Combining diverse training data enhances generalization. However, high computational latency and memory consumption remained significant hurdles for frontline mobile adoption.

2.2 Mobile Edge Vision Architectures

2.2.1 Depthwise Separable Convolutions & MobileNet Backbones

Introduction: Howard et al. [4] (IEEE ICCV, 2019) introduced MobileNetV3, combining hardware-aware neural architecture search (NAS), NetAdapt algorithms, and architectural novelties for efficient mobile computer vision.

Approach: MobileNetV3 employs depthwise separable convolutions that factorize standard convolution into a depthwise spatial filter and a 1 × 1 pointwise convolution. The architecture integrates linear bottleneck inverted residuals, Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) channel attention modules, and the computationally efficient Hard-Swish non-linear activation function:

Hard-Swish(x) = x · [ ReLU6(x + 3) / 6 ] (2.1)

Results: MobileNetV3 Large demonstrated a 3.2% accuracy improvement on ImageNet while reducing latency by 20% compared to MobileNetV2 on mobile CPU runtimes.

Relevance: MobileNetV3 provides the ideal architectural foundation for edge plant disease classification, offering rich representational capacity within a sub-20 MB parameter footprint.

2.2.2 Hard-Swish and Channel Attention Mechanisms

Introduction: Hu, Shen, and Sun [5] (IEEE CVPR, 2018) introduced Squeeze-and-Excitation networks, demonstrating that explicitly modeling channel-wise feature interdependencies significantly boosts representational power.

Approach: A global average pooling operation compresses spatial dimensions into a channel descriptor (squeeze), followed by two fully connected layers with a sigmoid gating mechanism (excitation) that scales feature channels dynamically based on diagnostic relevance.

Relevance to Plant Pathology: In foliar disease diagnosis, channel attention enables the neural network to suppress irrelevant green background pixels while dynamically amplifying chromatic channels corresponding to chlorotic halos, necrotic brown lesions, and powdery white spores.

2.3 Foliar Object Detection and Leaf ROI Extraction

2.3.1 YOLO Single-Shot Detectors for Complex Field Scenes

Introduction: Jocher et al. [6] (2024) developed the YOLO11 architecture, offering state-of-the-art real-time object detection through an enhanced backbone with C3k2 modules, Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF), and optimized anchor-free detection heads.

Approach: Rather than classifying full, uncurated field scenes containing extraneous soil and weeds, single-shot detectors predict spatial bounding box coordinates `(x, y, w, h)` to isolate individual leaf regions of interest (ROI).

Relevance: In agricultural environments, foliage is frequently surrounded by background clutter. Deploying a lightweight leaf detector (such as YOLO11n) ensures that downstream classification models focus exclusively on the biological leaf surface, filtering out background noise.

2.4.1 Controlled Laboratory Datasets: PlantVillage

Overview: PlantVillage [1] is the most widely utilized benchmark in agricultural computer vision, providing over 54,000 leaf photographs depicting 38 crop-disease categories. 2.4.2 In-Field Open Benchmark Studies: PlantDoc

Introduction: Singh, Jain, and Jain [7] (ACM COMPASS, 2020) presented PlantDoc, an open-source dataset designed specifically to address the laboratory-to-field domain shift.

Approach and Dataset: The authors curated 2,598 in-field leaf images across 13 plant species and 27 categories, characterized by diverse natural illumination, overlapping leaves, and complex agricultural backgrounds.

Results: Models trained purely on PlantVillage suffered a 31% to 52% absolute accuracy collapse when evaluated directly on PlantDoc field images. Incorporating in-field training imagery restored accuracy by up to 34%.

Conclusion: Controlled benchmarks produce brittle classifiers. Realistic agricultural decision-support systems must evaluate in-field datasets and implement robust preprocessing safeguards.

Without domain adaptation or input quality verification, deep learning models frequently latch onto non-pathological background artifacts.

2.5 Explainability and Confidence Calibration in Agronomic AI

Explainability: Ribeiro, Singh, and Guestrin [10] (ACM SIGKDD, 2016) introduced LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), which perturbs input superpixels to generate visual saliency heatmaps. In agronomic applications, visual explainability enables farmers to verify that predictions are driven by actual necrotic lesions rather than irrelevant stem shadows.

Confidence Calibration: Guo et al. [11] (ICML, 2017) demonstrated that modern deep neural networks with batch normalization and residual links are poorly calibrated, producing overconfident misclassifications. In agricultural decision support, uncalibrated confidence can lead farmers to apply costly and inappropriate chemical treatments. Applying probability thresholding (e.g., gating classifications below 60% confidence) provides an essential safety buffer.

2.7 Research Gap

The comprehensive synthesis of existing literature presented in Table 2.1 highlights three critical research and practical deployment gaps that motivate the design of the proposed platform:

  1. Modality and Crop Isolation vs. Multi-Crop Architecture: Existing agricultural diagnostic literature predominantly focuses on single-crop classification models or monolithic classifiers trained across undifferentiated plant species. Monolithic models suffer from severe inter-crop confusion (e.g., misclassifying healthy tomato foliage as diseased chilli leaves). 3.8 Machine Learning Methodology

The classification of plant leaf pathologies is formulated as a supervised multi-class visual recognition task. Let X ⊂ R3×224×224 denote the normalized image space and Y = {1, 2, ..., K} represent the set of discrete pathological categories for a given crop. The goal is to learn a parameterized mapping function fθ: X → ΔK−1 that outputs a probability simplex vector ŷ = [ŷ1, ..., ŷK]T.

Figure 3.9: Image preprocessing and tensor normalization flow

Loss Formulation: Neural parameters θ are optimized by minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss with label smoothing (ε = 0.1) to prevent overconfident boundary decisions on ambiguous foliar lesions:

LCE(θ) = −∑i=1K [ (1 − ε) yi + (ε / K) ] · ln(fθ(x)i)

Optimization Strategy: Training utilizes the AdamW optimizer with decoupled weight decay (λ = 10−4), initial learning rate η0 = 10−4, and momentum parameters β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999. Learning rate decay follows a cosine annealing trajectory with warm restarts:

ηt = ηmin + ½(η0 − ηmin) [ 1 + cos(πt / Tmax) ]

This schedule facilitates smooth convergence into broad, flat minima, enhancing out-of-distribution generalization when evaluated on real-world agricultural photographs.

3.10 Image Quality Inspection and Uncertainty Gating

A fundamental failure mode in deployed agricultural computer vision is silent misclassification: standard neural networks will gladly assign a 98% confidence score to an unreadable, blurry photograph or a picture of bare soil. To prevent farmer misdirection, the developed platform implements an automated quality inspection and uncertainty gating layer prior to model inference.

3.10.1 Laplacian Blur and Luminance Quality Metric

Image sharpness is computed on the greyscale image representation I(x,y) using the variance of the 2D Laplacian operator:

VarLap = (1 / HW) ∑x,y [ ∇2I(x,y) − μLap ]2,    where ∇2I = ∂2I/∂x2 + ∂2I/∂y2

If VarLap < 100.0, the image is flagged as motion-blurred or out-of-focus. Simultaneously, mean luminance L̄ = (1 / HW) ∑ (0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B) is evaluated against boundary thresholds $[40, 220]$ to reject underexposed (too dark) or washed-out overexposed frames.

3.11 Deep Learning Backbones & Inverted Residual Architecture

3.11.1 MobileNetV3 Compound Inverted Residual Blocks

The primary classification model utilizes the MobileNetV3-Large neural backbone, engineered specifically through hardware-aware Neural Architecture Search (NAS) for efficient mobile processor deployment [2]. The fundamental building block is the Inverted Residual Block with Squeeze-and-Excitation (MBConv-SE), illustrated in Figure 3.12.

Figure 3.12: MobileNetV3 inverted residual block with squeeze-and-excitation

The block operation proceeds in four distinct computation steps:

1. Pointwise Expansion: A 1 × 1 convolution expands input channel dimension Cin by expansion ratio t = 6 into a higher-dimensional feature manifold t · Cin.

2. Depthwise Convolution: A 3 × 3 or 5 × 5 depthwise spatial convolution applies spatial filtering independently per channel, dramatically reducing multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations compared to standard 2D convolutions.

3. Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) Attention: Global average pooling compresses spatial dimensions into a 1 × 1 × C descriptor, followed by two fully connected layers that compute channel-wise recalibration weights, dynamically emphasizing pathologically informative color bands.

4. Linear Bottleneck & Skip Connection: A final 1 × 1 projection compresses the channels back to Cout without non-linear activation to preserve manifold geometry. If $C_{ ext{in}} = C_{ ext{out}}$ and stride is 1, an identity skip connection adds input to output. Non-linearities utilize the computationally efficient Hard-Swish activation: $ ext{h-swish}(x) = x rac{ ext{ReLU6}(x+3)}{6}$.

3.11.2 Training Configuration and Transfer Learning

Training deep neural networks from random weight initializations on agricultural datasets leads to severe overfitting. To ensure robust feature representations, models were initialized with weights pre-trained on the 1.28-million image ImageNet-1k corpus, followed by two-stage transfer learning.

Stage 1 (Linear Probing): The convolutional backbone weights were frozen, and only the custom classification head was trained for 5 epochs with a learning rate of 10−3 to adapt to the new class space.

Stage 2 (Full Fine-Tuning): All backbone layers were unfrozen and trained end-to-end for 20 epochs with a reduced learning rate of 10−4 governed by cosine annealing. Extensive data augmentations were applied, including random rotations (±25°), horizontal/vertical flips, affine perspective shifts, and color jitter (brightness ±20%, contrast ±20%). 4.7 Discussion

The experimental findings support three primary conclusions regarding edge-native agricultural computer vision:

1. Feasibility of In-Browser Deep Inference: The compilation of deep architectures into optimized ONNX WebAssembly sessions proves that modern smartphones can execute multi-million parameter neural networks locally in 110–180 milliseconds. This eliminates cloud hosting costs and ensures uninterrupted functionality in rural offline regions.

2. Crucial Role of Pre-Inference Quality Filtering: Standard classifiers silently fail when presented with motion blur or illumination extremes. Incorporating client-side Laplacian sharpness checks effectively filters out corrupted tensors before inference, eliminating the leading source of field misclassification.

3. Domain Shift Awareness: Controlled laboratory models learn background cues. Grounding the system with a 60% confidence floor and margin thresholding prevents overconfident erroneous advisories, upholding agricultural safety.

4.8 Limitations

Despite promising functional performance, several engineering limitations must be acknowledged:

Single-Leaf Receptive Field: The model evaluates isolated leaf photographs and lacks canopy-level contextual information, such as overall plant wilting or soil moisture indicators.

Co-Infection Ambiguity: In real farming environments, plants frequently suffer simultaneous dual infections (e.g., early blight combined with spider mite stippling). The current multi-class single-label formulation cannot predict multi-pathogen combinations.

Mobile Memory Ceilings: Devices with less than 2 GB RAM may experience browser tab termination if background applications exhaust available WebAssembly memory heaps during model initialization.

CHAPTER 5

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

Foliar plant diseases pose continuous hazards to global food security and rural farm economics. In developing agrarian regions, smallholder farmers often lack prompt access to qualified agricultural extension pathologists, leading to delayed disease identification, misdiagnosed pathogen types, and indiscriminate application of broad-spectrum agrochemicals. While deep learning visual models have advanced rapidly in laboratory settings, their frontline adoption has been hindered by reliance on expensive cloud servers, brittle performance under unconstrained field conditions, and a lack of actionable agronomic guidance.

This project designed, implemented, and empirically evaluated an edge-deployable, multi-crop plant leaf disease classification and farmer advisory platform. Operating as a progressive web application directly within standard smartphone browsers, the system provides zero-latency foliar pathology recognition across three vital commercial crops: Tomato, Potato, and Chilli, spanning twenty-one discrete pathology and healthy foliage categories. By compiling deep convolutional architectures—including a fine-tuned MobileNetV3 V2 backbone with Squeeze-and-Excitation channel attention—into ONNX WebAssembly sessions, the platform achieves client-side execution latencies of 110–180 milliseconds without requiring server connectivity or data transmission.

To safeguard against field errors, the platform integrates automated Laplacian blur filtering, luminance bounding, and softmax uncertainty calibration with a 60% confidence gating floor. Predictions are paired with evidence-based agricultural management protocols covering cultural sanitation, biological remedies, and approved chemical options conforming to extension guidelines.

Future Work

Planned architectural enhancements include:

1. Expanded Botanical Scope: Training and integrating specialized classification backbones for additional staple regional crops, including Rice (Oryza sativa), Maize (Zea mays), and Banana (Musa).

2. In-Field Domain Adaptation: Implementing on-device continuous few-shot learning and test-time normalization to adapt feature extractors dynamically to local farm microclimates and camera sensors.

3. Multi-Pathogen Segmentation: Integrating lightweight YOLO or MobileSAM segmentation models to delineate individual lesion boundaries, quantifying foliar disease severity percentages rather than categorical labels alone.

4. Multimodal Audio Voice Guidance: Incorporating offline text-to-speech synthesis in regional vernacular languages (Malayalam, Hindi, Tamil) to maximize accessibility for illiterate farmers.
